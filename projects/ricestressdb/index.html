06 · Biological data infrastructure

The genes were known.
They just weren't queryable.

RiceStressDB organises stress-responsive rice genes with their functional annotations and the biotic and abiotic stress conditions they respond to, so that a question like "which transcription factors respond to both salinity and a pathogen?" becomes one query instead of a week of reading. Built with MySQL and PHP; registered as a software copyright associated with TNAU.

01 · The problem

Why a database and not a spreadsheet

Rice stress biology has a literature problem rather than a knowledge problem. The findings exist; they are distributed across thousands of papers, under inconsistent gene names, with no common vocabulary for the stress conditions themselves.

What makes this genuinely hard

  • Synonym chaos. One gene appears as OsNAC6, SNAC2, ONAC048, OsNAC2 and more across papers - the same entity under five names.
  • Two identifier systems. MSU (LOC_Os...) and RAP-DB (Os...g...) both in active use, and papers pick either.
  • Stress vocabulary is unstandardised. "Drought", "dehydration", "water deficit" and "osmotic stress" are used overlappingly.
  • Biotic and abiotic are studied separately - yet genes like OsNAC6 respond to both, which is exactly the interesting case.

Why a spreadsheet fails

A flat sheet forces one row per gene, so the many-to-many relationship between genes and stress conditions has to be crammed into a comma-separated cell. The moment it is, you cannot ask the only questions worth asking:

  • Which genes respond to both salinity and blast?
  • Which stress has the most characterised transcription factors?
  • Which genes are induced under one stress and repressed under another?

Those are relational questions. They need a relational model.

Design goal: make the gene-to-stress relationship a first-class object in the schema rather than a string inside a cell. Everything else in this project follows from that one decision.

The schema

Why synonyms get their own table

A gene has an unbounded number of historical names. Putting them in a child table means a search for SNAC2 and a search for OsNAC6 both resolve to the same gene_id - which is the single most useful thing the database does for a working biologist.

Why direction and evidence are on the relationship

"Responds to drought" is not a property of a gene. It is a property of the gene-stress pair, and it has a direction and a strength of evidence. Expression data and transgenic phenotype data are not the same claim, and the schema should not pretend they are.

The modelling judgement I would defend: direction and evidence belong on gene_stress_response, not on genes. Putting them on the gene would force one verdict per gene and silently destroy the cases where a gene is induced by one stress and repressed by another.

04 · The SQL

The queries that justify the schema

Three queries that are trivial in this model and painful in a flat file.

1. Genes responding to both an abiotic and a biotic stress

SELECT g.primary_symbol, g.msu_locus, g.gene_family,
       COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN s.category='abiotic' THEN s.stress_id END) AS abiotic_n,
       COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN s.category='biotic'  THEN s.stress_id END) AS biotic_n
FROM genes g
JOIN gene_stress_response r ON r.gene_id = g.gene_id
JOIN stress_conditions    s ON s.stress_id = r.stress_id
GROUP BY g.gene_id
HAVING abiotic_n > 0 AND biotic_n > 0
ORDER BY (abiotic_n + biotic_n) DESC;

2. Synonym-tolerant lookup

SELECT DISTINCT g.*
FROM genes g
LEFT JOIN gene_synonyms y ON y.gene_id = g.gene_id
WHERE g.primary_symbol LIKE CONCAT('%', ?, '%')
   OR g.msu_locus      LIKE CONCAT('%', ?, '%')
   OR g.rap_locus       LIKE CONCAT('%', ?, '%')
   OR y.synonym         LIKE CONCAT('%', ?, '%');

This is the query that makes the database usable. A biologist who read a 2005 paper searches SNAC2; a biologist who read a 2016 paper searches OsNAC6; both land on the same record.

3. Conditions ranked by how well characterised they are

SELECT s.stress_name, s.category,
       COUNT(DISTINCT r.gene_id) AS genes,
       SUM(r.evidence = 'transgenic') AS transgenic_evidence
FROM stress_conditions s
LEFT JOIN gene_stress_response r ON r.stress_id = s.stress_id
GROUP BY s.stress_id
ORDER BY genes DESC;

Counting transgenic evidence separately matters: a stress with fifty expression-only associations is less well understood than one with ten genes validated by transgenic phenotype, and a bare gene count hides that completely.

Point of the slide: the schema is not bookkeeping. It is what makes these three questions one-liners - and the third one is a research-planning question, not a lookup.
05 · Implementation

How it was built, and what I would change

The stack as built

  • MySQL for storage, with foreign keys and a unique constraint on (gene_id, stress_id)
  • PHP for the web interface - server-rendered search and browse pages
  • Indexes on msu_locus, rap_locus, primary_symbol, gene_synonyms.synonym and both foreign keys on the junction table
  • Curation from published literature into a staging sheet, then loaded

What I would do differently now

  • Controlled vocabulary for stress terms - map to Plant Ontology / PECO rather than free text, so "dehydration" and "water deficit" collapse properly
  • Full-text or trigram index on synonyms instead of LIKE '%...%', which cannot use a B-tree index and degrades as the table grows
  • A real API (JSON over HTTP) so the data is programmatically reachable, not only browsable
  • Provenance per row - curator, date and curation confidence, so records can be re-reviewed
  • Automated identifier validation against MSU/RAP releases, to catch retired loci

The performance problem I actually hit

Synonym search with a leading wildcard (LIKE '%NAC%') cannot use a standard index, so every query scanned the synonym table. At the project's scale that was invisible. It would not stay invisible, and knowing why - that a leading wildcard defeats a B-tree prefix match - is the part of this project I use most often elsewhere.

Honest assessment of the engineering: the data model is the strong part and I would defend it unchanged. The query layer was adequate for the scale and would need the fixes above before it grew.

Scale as built

  • 1,200+ gene records
  • 17 biotic and abiotic stress conditions
  • Functional annotations and stress metadata integrated per record

Those figures describe the production database at TNAU. The demo on slide 3 is a 15-record sample, and the repository contains that sample as SQL seed data - not the production content.

Attribution, stated precisely

  • Mine: the data model, the schema, the query layer, the web interface, and the curation work of reading papers and turning findings into structured records.
  • Not mine: the underlying biology. Every gene-stress association originates in a published study by other researchers. A curated database is a work of organisation, and its value depends on citing what it organises.
  • Institutional: developed in a TNAU research context during my B.Tech., with the registration associated accordingly.
Why I am this careful about it: curated databases are easy to overstate - "1,200 genes" can be made to sound like 1,200 discoveries. It is 1,200 records organised well, which is a real and different contribution.
07 · Sources

Where the sample records come from

Every record in the slide-3 sample is a real curated entry. Locus identifiers and stress associations were taken from these public resources and the primary literature they index.

  • funRiceGenes - curated collection of functionally characterised rice genes, the source for MSU/RAP identifier pairs, synonym sets and stress associations used in the sample: funricegenes.github.io. Individual records consulted include OsHKT1;5/SKC1, OsDREB1A, OsNAC6/SNAC2, SNAC1, OsLEA3, OsP5CS1, OsSOS1, OsNAC5 and Sub1B.
  • Oryzabase (National BioResource Project, Japan) - gene symbol synonyms, CGSNL nomenclature and MSU/RAP cross-references: shigen.nig.ac.jp/rice/oryzabase.
  • Locus identifiers for OsiSAP8 (cold), OsASR5 (drought), OsSAPK4 (salt) and OsEIL1 (submergence) - compiled table of key abiotic stress genes in rice, Recent molecular aspects and integrated omics strategies for understanding the abiotic stress tolerance of rice, PMC10221523.
  • SNAC1 drought phenotype (22-34% higher seed setting under severe reproductive-stage drought in the field, without yield penalty) - as recorded in funRiceGenes for LOC_Os03g60080.
  • Sub1 locus structure - three ERF genes (Sub1A, Sub1B, Sub1C) at the locus, with Sub1A the major determinant of submergence tolerance; Development of submergence-tolerant rice cultivars: the Sub1 locus and beyond, Annals of Botany (2009), as indexed by funRiceGenes.
  • MSU Rice Genome Annotation Project and RAP-DB - the two identifier systems the schema reconciles.
Why the sample is real rather than invented: a demo over fabricated gene records would show the interface and teach nothing, and anyone in the field would spot the fake identifiers immediately. Using genuine curated records means the cross-stress pattern the demo surfaces is a real feature of rice stress biology, not an artefact I built in.
