08 · Forest genomics

A tree worth more cut down
than standing. Markers change that.

Dalbergia latifolia - Indian rosewood, Bombay blackwood, sonokeling - is a premier timber species assessed as globally Vulnerable and listed on CITES Appendix II, with illegal logging driven by international demand. This project covered SSR and SNP marker discovery from genomic data, and the markers matter because they are what make a diversity claim, a provenance claim or a seizure identification testable.

01 · Context

Why this species, and why genomics

The conservation problem here is not obscurity. It is that the species is extremely valuable, slow-growing, and regenerates poorly - so demand outruns recovery.

Status and pressure

  • Native to low-elevation tropical monsoon forests of southern and eastern India; also present in Java and Nusa Tenggara, where it is known as sonokeling
  • Assessed as globally Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List, under criteria A1cd
  • Listed on CITES Appendix II
  • India has banned the export of logs and sawn wood from the species
  • Key threats in India: inadequate regeneration and slow growth against rising commercial demand

What genomic markers are actually for

  • Diversity assessment - is a remaining population genetically depauperate, or still diverse enough to be a seed source?
  • Provenance and traceability - DNA fingerprinting to test whether a timber consignment matches a claimed legal origin
  • Breeding and seed-source selection - identifying genetically distinct material for plantation programmes
  • Non-detriment findings - the baseline data CITES decisions require
The link worth making explicit: marker discovery is not an academic exercise bolted onto conservation. A CITES Appendix II listing creates a legal need to distinguish legal from illegal material, and that need is answered with genetics or it is not answered at all.
02 · The markers

SSRs and SNPs do different jobs

Both are variants. They are not interchangeable, and choosing between them is the first real decision in a marker project.

SSRs (microsatellites)SNPs
What they areShort tandem repeats - e.g. (AG)₁₂ - varying in repeat number between individualsSingle-base differences at a defined position
Information per locusHigh - multi-allelic, often many alleles per locusLow - usually two alleles
Number availableThousandsHundreds of thousands to millions
AssayPCR with flanking primers, size separation - cheap per sample, no sequencing neededSequencing or genotyping array - higher setup cost, cheaper at scale
Best forParentage, individual identification, population structure on a modest budget, field-deployable traceabilityGenome-wide diversity, selection scans, fine-scale structure, high-throughput work
WeaknessHomoplasy - identical allele sizes with different mutational histories; genotyping requires care across labsNeeds reference resources and compute; each locus carries little information alone

For a species where enforcement labs in range states may need to run assays cheaply and repeatably, SSRs remain genuinely practical. A handful of well-chosen SSR loci can individually identify a tree, which is exactly what a timber-provenance case requires - and the assay needs a thermocycler, not a sequencer.

Project reasoning: discover both from genomic data, but prioritise SSRs for deployability. The best marker is not the most informative one in principle; it is the most informative one the people who need it can actually run.

That is why tools like MISA require them to be declared up front.
04 · From repeat to marker

What happens after the scan

Finding a repeat is the easy part. A usable marker needs several more steps, each of which eliminates candidates.

Step 1Detect perfect repeats above threshold (the tool on slide 3)
Step 2Require sufficient unique flanking sequence on both sides
Step 3Design primers in the flanks - Primer3, with Tm and GC constraints
Step 4Check primer specificity against the genome; discard multi-locus hits
Step 5Wet-lab validation - amplification success across individuals
Step 6Polymorphism screening on a diversity panel

The attrition is severe, and that is normal

Most detected repeats never become markers. Candidates are lost because flanking sequence is repetitive and primers cannot be placed uniquely; because primers amplify multiple loci in a genome with a high repeat fraction; because amplification fails or is inconsistent; and - most often - because the locus turns out to be monomorphic in the populations of interest. A marker that does not vary tells you nothing, however clean the assay.

SNP discovery in parallel

The same genomic data supports variant calling: align reads to the assembly, call variants, then filter hard on depth, quality, missingness and allele balance. Filtering is where SNP projects are won or lost - an unfiltered call set is mostly alignment artefacts around repeats and indels.

Why a reference matters so much here

Marker development is far easier with a genome assembly: you can check primer uniqueness, place markers in physical space, and distinguish paralogues from alleles. A D. latifolia de-novo assembly has since been reported - 22,319 contigs totalling 613.95 Mb with high completeness - and for the related D. sissoo a chromosome-scale assembly of 660.37 Mb across 10 pseudochromosomes with 29,737 protein-coding genes.

What I would tell myself at the start of this project: budget for attrition from the beginning. Report "n loci screened, m polymorphic" rather than the number of repeats the scan found, because the first number is work and the second is the only one that matters.
05 · Application

Markers as an enforcement tool

This is the part of the project I found most compelling, and it is a straightforwardly operational argument.

The enforcement problem

A CITES Appendix II listing means trade is permitted under permit. Enforcement therefore depends on verifying claims: that a consignment is the species declared, and that it came from where the paperwork says. Visual timber identification can reach genus but struggles with species, and says nothing at all about geographic origin.

What genetics adds

  • Species identification from wood, including processed material
  • Population assignment - testing a sample against reference populations for a claimed origin
  • Individual matching - linking a seized log to a specific stump

All three require a reference database of genotyped material from known locations. Indonesia has pursued exactly this: a non-detriment findings report combined with a DNA database for D. latifolia in Java and West Nusa Tenggara.

The honest limitation: none of this works without reference sampling. A marker panel with no georeferenced reference genotypes can confirm species but cannot test an origin claim. The bottleneck in timber forensics is almost never the markers - it is the fieldwork behind the reference database, which is slow, expensive and unglamorous.
Why this project sits in my portfolio: it is where I first saw a bioinformatics output land directly on a decision someone has to make - a customs officer with a container and a document that may or may not be true.
